# 51 — Company-level walk-forward study: model individual ASX companies from the past, predict, then compare with what actually happened

Date: 2026-11-14. Status: research report on unlicensed public data, not a card run under RND-025. Research use only.
Companion file with every table: `51_company_predict_vs_actual_tables.md`. Code, data manifest, trial log and hashes: `scratchpad/company_study/` (section 12).

Everything in the tables is generated by code from a pre-registration file that was hashed before any return was computed (SHA-256 `6729ff1d8ac8d021…`). Changes made after the hash are listed in section 1.6 and marked "[added]" wherever they appear.

## 0. Answer first

**No model here predicts company winners well enough, after costs and tax, to beat simply holding the index at this account size. One family of rules ("steady winners": momentum + near the 52-week high + low volatility) shows a small, consistent lean in the right direction that is not statistically proven. Buying the ten or four "hottest" momentum stocks, which is what most people mean by picking winners, lost badly.**

The test: at every December from 2000 to 2023 (and at every month-end as a check), using only prices up to that day, rank the 300 most-traded ASX companies with each model, write down the predicted top tenth, bottom tenth and "top 10 winners", then look at what those stocks actually did over the next 12 months. Delisted companies are kept.

What this means, in one paragraph. Stock prices do carry a little usable information: stocks that have been rising steadily, are near their highs and are not wild tend to do slightly better than average, and stocks that have collapsed or are extremely volatile tend to do worse. But the information is spread thinly across 30 or more names and shows up as an average over many years. It does not tell you which four or ten companies will be the next NEXTDC. A A$5,000 account can hold only about four stocks, and four stocks chosen by any of these rules behave like a lottery ticket: the outcome is dominated by luck, the worst years are far worse than the index, and the popular version (buy what went up most) destroyed most of the money. The index already holds every future winner and lets it grow without a trade or a tax bill. At the owner's size (A$5,000 + A$500 a month) the plan in A200/BGBL, never sold, beat or matched every pre-registered stock model once dividends, costs and tax are counted. The one model that came close did better than Australian shares alone in most start years, on weak evidence and a generous dividend assumption, and still trailed 40/60 in ten of twelve comparisons.

## 0.1 Caveats that change how far to trust this

- **Unlicensed, imperfect data.** Prices to May 2024 come from a hobbyist's public GitHub copy of ASX end-of-day files (no licence stated). I found and removed 3,888 bad ticks, repaired about 1,400 splits and consolidations and 70 demergers or capital returns, and dropped one corrupt code. Errors certainly remain (section 2). They add noise to every model; I found no sign they favour one.
- **Price-only returns.** The data have no dividends. Stock returns and the index are both shown price-only (like for like). Dividends matter to the conclusion: the index paid 4.3% a year, plain-momentum picks about 1.9%, so price-only results *flatter* momentum by about 2 points a year (Table 6.3, estimated from surviving companies only).
- **24 yearly observations.** Each December cohort is one observation. The every-month check uses 277 overlapping cohorts, which is not 277 pieces of evidence; treated honestly it is worth about the same as one yearly series (Table 4.2).
- **The model list is hindsight.** Momentum, the 52-week high and low volatility were known by 2000 only in part. Choosing them in 2026 flatters them; they still did not pass.
- **Delistings.** A company that stops trading is sold at its last traded price. For real failures that is too kind (Dick Smith's last price equals its entry price). Sensitivities at −30% and −100% are in Table 4.4; they lower every model by 1 to 4 points and change no conclusion.
- **Stocks delisted after May 2024 have no later price** (the extension source carries only companies still listed). 15 of 300 stocks in the December 2023 cohort exit at their May 2024 price. Cohorts formed after May 2024 are survivor-only and are excluded from every average.
- **No sector, size or fundamental data.** "Size" is traded value, not market value. Sector is known only for companies still listed. Quality and profitability could not be tested without survivorship and look-ahead bias (section 1.6).
- **Tax is approximate.** 32% on net realised gains each year, with and without the 50% discount; dividend tax and franking are ignored on both sides, which favours the stock side slightly less than the index (the index's franking is worth about 1.1–1.4% a year on the Australian holding, report 39).
- **In this data NEXTDC closed at A$17.79 on 31 May 2024 and A$10.03 on 13 November 2026**, after a 42% fall between consecutive price rows on 21 October 2026 (five days apart). The case study uses those prices as given.

### 1.2 Universe and timing

- Securities: three-character codes that trade, excluding index codes and exchange-traded products (list built from ASX's monthly investment-products report before any return was computed). Listed investment companies and property trusts stay in.
- A gap of more than 200 days in a code starts a new company (code reuse). Code changes are linked using the ASX change list, and by a price-and-volume continuity rule where the list does not reach (255 links, e.g. WPL→WDS, Z1P→ZIP, CTX→ALD).
- At each formation date: companies with at least 200 trading days in the last 252, ranked by median daily traded value over those days. Main universe: the top 300. Checks: top 200, and top 500 (used for the case studies, because NXT and others started small).
- Signals at the formation-day close; buy at the next day's close; sell at the close of the day after the next formation date. The index is measured over the same days. Nothing after the formation date enters a signal or the universe.

### 1.3 Corporate actions

- Splits and consolidations: for the 530 codes with stockanalysis history, the step in (raw close ÷ split-adjusted close). For everything else, a rule on the raw series (clean ratio, no panic volume, no reversal), plus rules for odd-ratio consolidations and capital returns added during cleaning. Scored against stockanalysis: 85% of the rule's events are confirmed, and it finds 78% of stockanalysis's large steps (Table 2.2). Companies that delisted before 2024 rely on the rule alone, so roughly one in five of their splits is probably missed.
- Demergers and large capital returns (worth 15% or more of the price) are folded into the price where stockanalysis has dividend-adjusted data (63 events from 2016), plus seven older events entered by hand from memory [unverified]: WMC/Alumina 2002, Mayne/Symbion 2005, Toll/Asciano 2007, Tabcorp/Echo 2011, Australian Infrastructure Fund 2013, UGL 2014, Brambles 2001. Smaller or older demergers (BHP/South32, Amcor/Orora, Foster's/Treasury, CSR/Rinker, AMP/HHG …) are **not** adjusted and understate the parent's return that year.
- Dividends are not in the data. Section 6.3 estimates them.

### 1.4 Models

All fixed before the first run. Higher score = predicted winner. No parameter was tuned.

### 1.5 What is measured

Per model and date: rank IC (Spearman correlation of score with the next 12-month return); the predicted top tenth and bottom tenth (30 stocks each) and top 10, top 4 and top 20; hit rates (share of the predicted top 10 that land in the actual top tenth or fifth, in the bottom tenth, or lose more than half); top minus bottom; top minus XJO; top minus the equal-weight universe. Then costs (IBKR: A$6.60 minimum or 0.088%; half-spread 0.10% for the 100 most traded, 0.25% otherwise, never less than half a tick), three account sizes (A$5,000 = 4 stocks, A$25,000 = 10, A$100,000 = 20), tax, persistence, walk-forward model choice, and the owner's contribution plan.

Pre-declared pass test: mean top-tenth-minus-XJO above zero with t ≥ 3.0 on the December cohorts, positive after costs at A$25,000, positive in both halves, June cohorts agreeing in sign.

### 1.6 Deviations from the frozen pre-registration (all in `PREREG.md` section 9)

Data cleaning, decided by looking at one-day price moves and split lists, never at model results: the split rule was tightened three times (it first flagged 13,000 one-tick moves in sub-5-cent stocks); bad ticks, demergers and odd-ratio consolidations were handled as in 1.3; delisting keywords were narrowed; cohorts after May 2024 were excluded as survivor-only; four entries were removed by hand from the extreme-winner and blow-up *lists* only (Herald Resources, Neon, Australian Infrastructure Fund, AMCIL: artefacts of thin trading or capital returns). Model statistics were never hand-edited.

Additions requested by the owner part-way through, written into the file **before any result table had been read** but after the hash, so each is labelled "[added]" and counted in the trial total: models M11–M16; every month-end as a start date; four sub-periods; delisting scenarios; matched-size placebo; deflated Sharpe ratio and probability of backtest overfitting; the lifecycle comparison against 40/60 index funds; two more look-alike screens; the 30 biggest winners and blow-ups with readings 6, 3 and 1 months before.

Considered and **not** added: quality or profitability models. Fundamentals are reachable only for companies still listed today, as restated, without the dates they became known. A back-test on them would be biased twice over (survivors only, and hindsight in the numbers). Low volatility (M5) and return consistency (M16) are the only price-based stand-ins.

Trial log: `out/trials.csv`, 303 rows (155 pre-registered, 147 added, 1 control). Every run is there, including the ones that looked bad.

Reading it:

- **The rank IC column is the good news.** Momentum-type scores line up with next-year returns in three years out of four or better. For the 52-week high the t-statistic is 3.8.
- **The "top − XJO" column is what an investor would earn, and it is weak.** The predicted top tenth of plain momentum beat the index by 2.7 points a year with t = 0.5, in 13 of 24 years. The pre-registered combination M9 managed +4.9 points, t = 1.9, 16 of 24 years.
- **The last column explains the gap.** The *median* stock in momentum's top tenth lost 1.4% a year. The average is carried by a few big winners; the typical pick was a loser. M9's median pick made 6.3%.
- **Most of the information is on the short side.** Bottom tenths returned −6% to +1% a year. A long-only account cannot use that except by not owning those stocks, which the index mostly manages anyway because they are small.
- **Low volatility, small size and both reversal rules did not work** as long-only stock pickers on price returns. One-month reversal was reliably wrong (rank IC negative in 75% of years).

## 10. Learnings the owner can work through

1. **"Can a model spot the next NEXTDC?" No — and NEXTDC itself shows why.** For its first six listed years NXT looked average on every measure. Models noticed it in 2017 at A$6, after it had nearly quadrupled, and then it went sideways for two years. The 135 companies that looked most like it had a median five-year result of −18%.
2. **Models can tilt the odds across many stocks; they cannot name the few.** The scores carry real information (rank IC about +0.15), worth perhaps 3–5 points a year across a 30-stock tenth before costs and unproven statistically. Across 4 or 10 stocks the luck band is ±5 to ±9 points a year over 24 years, wider than any edge found.
3. **A A$5,000 account is a 4-stock account.** Four stocks picked by the popular rule (biggest 12-month gainers) fell 77% in their worst year and lost 13% a year overall. The index's worst year was −41%.
4. **The typical "winner" pick is a loser.** The average return of momentum's picks was positive; the median pick lost money. You get the average only by holding all of them every year, including the years it is 25 points behind.
5. **What looked safest was not.** The calmest stocks of 2007 included listed property trusts that then lost 96–99%. Low volatility lagged the price index by 2 points a year; its dividends recover part of that.
6. **Momentum finds winners late and leaves late.** It joined 28 of the 30 biggest runs, a median 8 months and +239% in. It also held 8 of the 30 biggest blow-ups as they began, and a yearly rule exits up to a year after the top (Appen: −56% before the sell).
7. **You cannot pick the model from its track record.** The best-so-far model changed seven times in 19 years and returned 4.5 points a year *less* than the index after promising 8 more. Report 48 found the same for asset-allocation rules.
8. **Costs are survivable; tax and turnover are the real drag; concentration is the killer.** Yearly turnover of 96–98% means every gain is realised. At A$5,000 the steadier lists cost about 1 point a year to trade and another 1–1.5 in tax.
9. **Dividends are half the index's return and momentum stocks pay about half the index's yield.** Any stock-picking result quoted on prices alone overstates momentum by about 2 points a year. Franking credits widen the gap further in the index's favour.
10. **Most companies lose to the index.** Over five years 38% of top-300 companies beat it, 26% lost more than half and under 2% rose fivefold. The index return *is* those few; holding the index is the only way to be sure of owning them, and A200/BGBL holds NEXTDC, Pro Medicus, WiseTech and their overseas equivalents already.
11. **Survivorship fools every shortcut.** A screen run on today's company list had a 3% failure rate; the same screen on companies since delisted had 11%. Famous case studies are the survivors.
12. **The owner's plan needed no decisions and was hard to beat.** A$5,000 + A$500 a month into 40/60 index funds from 2000 reached about A$543,000. The best pre-registered stock model roughly matched that at the owner's size, and beat it only for someone who already had A$25,000–100,000 invested in 2000; from every later start it was 2–22% behind. That took a generous dividend assumption, several hundred trades and a capital-gains calculation every year. The popular rule ended below the amount paid in. Part of 40/60's lead is world shares, which no ASX stock rule can supply.
13. **If the urge to pick stocks remains**, the evidence here says: keep it outside the plan, small enough to lose entirely, spread over at least 20–30 names, avoid stocks below their 10-month average and stocks that have just spiked, and expect to trail the index in about 4 years out of 10. None of that is a recommendation to do it.

## 11. Limits of this study, in order of importance

1. Unlicensed third-party prices with residual corporate-action errors; companies delisted before 2024 depend on a split rule with about 78% recall.
2. No dividends in the main results; the dividend estimate is from survivors, 2016 onward.
3. 24 independent yearly observations; one market; one 24-year period that contains two crashes and a mining boom.
4. Demergers before 2016 mostly unadjusted (understates some large companies in single years); seven adjusted from memory.
5. Delisting returns approximated; takeover premiums are captured only up to the last traded price.
6. Traded value stands in for size; no sector history; no fundamentals.
7. The tax model ignores dividend tax, franking, whole-share rounding and the timing of tax payments. In the lifecycle table the after-tax path can end above the before-tax path for one row because tax payments change which size band the account is in; treat differences of a few percent between those columns as noise.
8. The equal-weight comparison uses the cap-weighted XJO as "the index"; the equal-weight universe averaged within 1 point of XJO a year, so this is not driving results.
