# 48 — Walk-forward study: if the model had been chosen from the past, would the same model, and the promised result, have turned up afterwards?

Date: 2026-11-14. Spec: `FD_FINAL.md` v2.10 (VAL-20 to VAL-23, RND-025, PRT-10, PRT-23, SIZ-15). Builds on reports 36 and 38 and their independent replications 45 and 46.
Status: research report, not a card run under RND-025. Same free public proxy data as report 38.

Every table below is generated by code in `scratchpad/walkforward/code/` from the tournament's own frozen data and contestant code (`strategies.py` SHA-256 `3526217e…`, unchanged; `engine.py` unchanged). Nothing in reports 36, 38, 45 or 46 was modified.

## 0. Answer first

**No. Picking the best model from the past did not give the same model at each date, and it did not give the promised result afterwards. Holding static 40/60 throughout did better than every selection rule tested.**

The test: stand at the end of 1990, 1995, 2000, 2005, 2010, 2015, 2020 and 2023 with only the data that existed then. Run the report-38 tournament. Pick the winner. Then see what that pick did in the following five years, and to September 2026, against static 40/60.

What this means, in one paragraph. A backtest ranks contestants by return, but return rankings on 15 to 48 years of data are mostly noise plus one real effect: the share of the portfolio in shares. Risk rankings are real and repeat. So "learning" from past returns moves money toward whatever the last period favoured (world shares in 2000, Australian shares in 2010) just before it stops working, and learning from past Sharpe moves money into bond-heavy rules that give up 3 points a year. The spec's present rules (no fitted parameters, no return claim without t ≥ 3, static 40/60 as the default) would have made the right call at every cut-off. This study supports keeping them, and adds two specific tightenings in section 6.

## 0.1 Caveats that change how far to trust this

- **Seven independent windows.** Five-year windows from 1991 to 2025 give seven non-overlapping tests. Winning 2 of 7 is not statistically different from a coin (probability 0.23 of 2 or fewer if the true rate were one half). The yearly cut-offs (36) overlap and add little independent evidence. The conclusion rests on the consistency of the picture across rules, panels and placebo tests, not on any single count.
- **The contestant list itself is hindsight.** The 47 rules were written in 2026. A researcher in 1990 could not have known GEM (2014), Keller's rules (2014–2018) or the CAPE (1988, barely). Using data only to the cut-off removes look-ahead in the numbers, not in the choice of what to test. This flatters the dynamic rules, and they still did not win.
- **Short history at the early cut-offs.** The 1990 cut-off has 15 years of data (1976–1990), a period of 18% nominal returns and one crash. That is what a researcher then would have had.
- **Proxy data, monthly, same-bar fills, approximate tax model**: all of report 38's section 0.1 applies unchanged. Regime-B tax is applied to all of history. 2026-01 to 2026-09 uses flagged proxies for Australian shares and global bonds.
- **Forward results use a fresh A$25,000 account opened the month after the cut-off**, held at constant size. That is how a real switch would start. It is why 40/60's 2024–2025 figure here (36.3%) differs from report 38's holdout figure (38.2%), which continued an account whose weights had drifted.
- **The 2024–2026 window was already read** in report 38. It is reported here as the last forward window, not as new evidence.
- **One contrary result is reported in section 4.6**: on the independent replication's data, picking by pre-tax return chose dual momentum (GEM) every time and it kept a 1.2-point edge afterwards. On the tournament's data the same rule lagged. The difference is the pre-1990 world-share series, as report 46 found.

## 1. Method

**Data and contestants.** `algo_tournament/data/derived/returns.csv` and `macro.csv` (read-only), monthly, AUD, net of fund fees, 1975-01 to 2026-09. The 47 contestants of report 38 with their frozen parameters. Main comparison: the 39 with history from 1976-01. Secondary: all 47 on the common window from 1991-01.

**No peeking.** Every contestant's weights for month *t*+1 use data to month-end *t*. I tested this directly: the weights were rebuilt from data files cut off at each of the eight dates and compared with the full-data weights up to that date. Maximum absolute difference across all 47 contestants and all eight cut-offs: 0.0 (`out/t0_invariance.json`). That covers the expanding-window pieces (trend forecast scalars, the yearly HMM refit, the CAPE percentile, the volatility thresholds). At the 2023-12 cut-off my in-sample numbers equal report 38's `metrics.csv` to the last digit for all 47 contestants (after-tax CAGR, net CAGR, Sharpe, maximum drawdown, A$5,000 after-tax CAGR).

**Cut-offs.** Year-end 1990, 1995, 2000, 2005, 2010, 2015, 2020 and 2023 (the main grid). Every year-end from 1985 to 2025 was also run, for the overlapping statistics.

**Selection rules.** At each cut-off, on data from 1976-01 to the cut-off:

| Rule | Picks the contestant with the highest… |
|---|---|
| A | CAGR after costs and Regime-B tax at 32% with a full sale at the cut-off, A$25,000 (report 38's leaderboard rule) |
| B | CAGR after costs, before tax, A$25,000 |
| C | Sharpe ratio over cash, after costs, A$25,000 |
| D | Rule A's measure among contestants whose in-sample maximum drawdown is 20% or less |
| E | Rule A's measure among contestants whose in-sample maximum drawdown is at most two-thirds of 40/60's |
| F | CAGR ÷ maximum drawdown |
| G | Rule A's measure at A$5,000 (the owner's actual starting size) |

**Forward accounting.** A fresh A$25,000 account from the month after the cut-off, run with report 38's engine (IBKR commission, spreads, A$1,000 minimum order, constant size), for 60 months and to 2026-09, before tax and after Regime-B tax with a full sale at the end. Static 40/60 is run the same way over the same window. "Edge" always means contestant minus 40/60, in points of CAGR a year.

**Luck benchmarks.** (1) A random pick: the average forward edge of all 38 alternatives. (2) A time-shift placebo: each non-static rule's history of holdings is rotated in time by a random amount, which keeps what it holds on average and how often it trades but removes any timing information; 1,000 sets. (3) A zero-edge world: each contestant is 40/60 plus its own active-return noise with the mean removed; 1,000 sets.

**Gates.** The spec's two claim families (VAL-20), simplified: a t-statistic on net active return against a 40/60-plus-cash blend of equal volatility (pass mark 3.0), and a paired stationary-bootstrap test of the maximum-drawdown difference against 40/60 (10% level).

**Fitted parameters.** The tournament fits nothing (VAL-22). To test fitting, two families were tuned at each cut-off: L1 with 80 settings of its three look-backs, and GEM with look-backs of 3 to 18 months.

**Projections.** Section 5.

**Run size.** 1,847 contestant-by-cut-off records, each with up to ten simulations; 96 parameter settings at 39 cut-offs; 2,000 placebo sets; 324 projections of 10,000 paths.

Reading it:

- **Rule A never settles.** CAPE de-risking in 1990 (its worst drawdown to that date was 19% against 28% for 40/60), world shares in 1995 and 2000 (the US boom), a momentum sleeve in 2005, Australian shares in 2010 (the mining boom), the momentum sleeve since. Each pick is the asset mix that the preceding years favoured.
- **The margins are tiny.** The top six under rule A were within 0.9 points of each other in 1990, 1.5 in 2000, 0.8 in 2010 and 0.5 in 2023. Static 40/60 was 7th, 4th, 4th and 4th. Table 8 shows none of these gaps comes near statistical significance.
- **The Sharpe rule is stable for the wrong reason.** It picks risk parity + trend every time because that rule holds mostly bonds and cash, and low volatility is persistent. It is a stable choice of asset mix, not a stable finding of skill.
- **The in-sample ranking barely moves between cut-offs** (rank correlation 0.96–1.00 for after-tax CAGR, bottom of Table 5), because each window contains the previous one. A stable ranking in overlapping data says nothing about the future.

## 6. Conclusions in plain English

**Does picking a model from the past give the same model later?** No. Under the tournament's own rule the "best" model changed four times across eight dates, and each choice was the asset mix that had just had a good run. Only the Sharpe rule kept one pick, and it kept it because bond-heavy portfolios are always smoother.

**Does it give the promised results?** No for return, yes for risk ordering.
- The return edge over 40/60 shown in the backtest (about half a point a year) turned into a deficit of 0.7 to 1.3 points. A blind draw did about as badly.
- Absolute returns came in at roughly half to three-fifths of the backtest figure (rule A's picks 13.7% promised, 6.5% delivered; 40/60 13.2% and 7.8%), because the early data came from a high-inflation era.
- Which contestants are less risky was predicted well. How deep the next drawdown would be was not: 40/60 fell further in 2008 than at any time in the data before it.

**What this implies for the system's learning and promotion rules.**

1. **Keep VAL-20's return gate as it is.** At no cut-off in 35 years would any of the 38 alternatives have reached t = 3.0. The gate would have blocked every return-based promotion, and blocking was correct. A looser rule ("promote the leaderboard winner", or "promote if ahead by X over N years") would have rotated the account through CAPE timing, world shares, Australian shares and a momentum sleeve, at a cost of about 1.3 points a year plus the tax on each switch, which this study does not count.
2. **Keep VAL-22 (no fitted parameters).** Refitting L1's or GEM's look-backs as data arrives would have cost 0.1 to 0.9 points a year against leaving them alone. A challenger that differs from the live rule only by a tuned parameter should not be eligible for promotion.
3. **Drawdown claims are the only kind worth registering, and they should be priced.** Table 8 shows the drawdown gate works. It also shows the price: 2.5 to 4.4 points a year after tax in windows without a crash. A card that passes Family A should state the expected return given up, taken from the non-crash windows, next to the drawdown gained. Suggested addition to the G2 memo: the Table 8 pair (drawdown gain, return cost) for the candidate, by five-year window.
4. **Add a walk-forward stability check to the robustness battery (RND-026).** Proposed wording: "For any card that claims a ranking against B2, rerun the selection at five-year cut-offs using data to each cut-off only. Report whether the candidate would have been chosen at each, and its forward result. A candidate that is chosen at fewer than half the cut-offs, or whose forward edge has the opposite sign to its in-sample edge on average, makes no return claim." This is cheap (the code exists in `scratchpad/walkforward/code/`) and it is the test the owner asked for.
5. **Sharpe is not evidence** (SIZ-15 already says so). Here it selected the same bond-heavy rule every time and that rule trailed 40/60 by 3 points a year after tax over the five-year windows, and by 2.5 to 6.4 points measured from each cut-off to 2026.
6. **Challengers in paper trading.** A few years of paper results will rank the challengers by whatever shares, bonds and gold did in those years. Table 5 says that ranking has no forecasting value for return. VAL-25 ("no performance conclusion is drawn from paper trading") is right and should stay.

**What this implies for the owner's plan.**

1. **Static 40/60 with regular contributions is the choice this evidence supports.** It was never the top of any backtest and it beat the top of every backtest afterwards, on average. Not being the winner in hindsight is normal and is not a reason to switch.
2. **Do not move toward whichever mix led recently.** The near-ties at the top (37/63, the momentum sleeve, 100% of one region) are noise at ±0.2 points or a regional bet at ±5 points that reverses.
3. **L1 remains what the spec says it is: a drawdown overlay with a cost.** Held throughout, the cash-ETF version cut the drawdown in 5 of 7 windows (41% to 8% in 2006–2010) and trailed 40/60 by 0.3 points a year on average after tax, with −4 to −5 points in 1991–1995 and 2016–2020. The spec version with idle cash at 0% trailed by 0.7. That is the H1/H11 trade, unchanged by this study.
4. **Use the wealth projection as a range with a soft floor.** Plan on method B's figures. Treat the 10th percentile as something that has been breached about one time in four, not one in ten, and always because a crash fell near the end of the window. Nothing in 40 years landed above the 90th percentile, so the top of the band is not a planning number.
5. **Expect a drawdown deeper than the worst one in the backtest at some point.** It has happened once already in this data.

## 7. What would change these conclusions

- A contestant that is chosen at most cut-offs **and** keeps a positive forward edge on both the tournament data and the replication data. GEM does this on one data set only.
- Licensed index data (RND-024) that moves the pre-1990 world-share series enough to change the GEM result in Table 11.
- More independent windows. Seven is few; each further five years adds one.
