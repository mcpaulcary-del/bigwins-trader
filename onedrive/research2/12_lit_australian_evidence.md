## 2. What the evidence means

### (a) ASX versus global exposure, and hedging

- **Returns do not justify home bias.** Australian equities returned about 6% real over a century (#1, #2). Over the last 30 years they were ahead of unhedged international shares (9.1% versus 8.2%) and behind US shares (11.1%) (#3). Over the last five years VAS returned 8.72% against VGS at 12.53% (#4).
- **Concentration is structural.** Financials plus resources have been about half the market since 1917 (#1). An ASX 200 ETF is a two-sector bet with roughly half its weight in ten names.
- **Unhedged global equity has cushioned crises.** The RBA states the mechanism directly (#7), and practitioner data for 2008–09 and March 2020 agree (#8).
- **The cushion is not reliable.** The correlation has fallen (#8), and in April 2025 the US dollar weakened while equities fell (#9). The year to September 2026 shows the cost of being unhedged when the Australian dollar rises: VGAD 17.2% against VGS 10.2% (#4).
- **Long-run hedged and unhedged returns are close.** Hedged 9.3% against unhedged 8.9% (#8, one source). Treat hedging as a risk choice, not a return source. Large super funds hedge only about 25% of international equities (#7).
- **Franking: the sources measure different things.** The market prices credits at about 35% of face value (#10, #11). A resident individual who passes the 45-day rule receives 100% of face value, worth about 1.5% a year on an ASX 200 holding (#12, one source). That is a real after-tax tilt toward Australian shares, but it is smaller than the recent return gap to global shares.

### (b) Are trend and momentum rules on ASX instruments supported?

- **Cross-sectional stock momentum in large caps: more evidence for than against.** Four published studies find it in ASX 200 or big stocks (#13, #14, #15, #19). Spreads reduce it (#13), the winner side carries it (#14), and mining names damage it (#16).
- **The evidence is contested.** One study finds only short-term contrarian beats buy-and-hold (#16). An unpublished paper says the effect is a look-ahead artefact (#18). The one live product, MTUM, returned -4.74% over the year to September 2026 against about +3% for the index (#4); one year proves little.
- **Short-term reversal and the 52-week high do not survive costs in liquid names** (#17, #20).
- **Time-series trend on ASX ETFs has no Australian peer-reviewed support that I could find.** Any such rule in the spec rests on international evidence and your own backtest.

### (c) Realistic expectations for retail systematic traders

- **Leveraged retail trading loses.** Between 57% and 72% of retail CFD clients lost money in every ASIC dataset from 2017 to FY24, with fees a visible share of losses (#21, #22).
- **Retail short-term timing in 2020 was poor** (#23). There are no Australian data on retail algorithmic traders specifically.
- **The measurable edge is cost and discipline.** Core ETFs trade at 2–4 basis points (#4). Expect market-like returns, with any momentum premium uncertain.

## 3. Decisions for the spec

1. **Core allocation.** Make global equity the larger equity sleeve and cap Australian equity at a stated home-bias weight; I suggest 30–40% of equities. Record the franking benefit (about 1.5% a year, one source) as the reason for any overweight.
2. **Hedging.** Default the global sleeve to unhedged, with a configurable hedged share of 0–50%; I suggest starting at 25–30%, consistent with super-fund practice. Do not model the Australian dollar as a guaranteed crisis hedge. Stress-test with the currency flat or rising during an equity fall.
3. **Instruments.** Restrict the first universe to ETFs with reported spreads of 0.04% or less and deep daily turnover. Read spreads from the monthly ASX report each month and drop any ETF above the threshold. Treat anything at 0.07% or wider as satellite only.
4. **Execution window.** Never send marketable orders in the first two minutes after the open; I suggest a five-minute blackout. Use either limit orders in continuous trading from mid-morning to mid-afternoon, or the closing auction.
5. **Closing auction.** Using the close matches backtests built on closing prices and reaches about 23% of daily value. Handle the auction ending by 16:10:30 and the Post Close session to 16:21:30, where unfilled orders can still execute at the auction price. I did not check which IBKR order types support this; test it in paper trading.
6. **Global ETF caveat.** Close-auction liquidity figures are market-wide, not ETF-specific. Global ETFs trade while their underlying markets are closed, and I found no data on their auction quality. Compare auction fills with net asset value in paper trading before relying on the close for them.
7. **Price handling.** Do not hard-code tick sizes. The ETF tick schedule may change for prices between $2 and $5.
8. **Momentum.** If included, limit it to ASX 200 constituents or liquid ETFs, long-only, with 6–12 month formation and monthly or slower rebalancing. Require the backtest to use point-in-time constituents including delisted stocks, and to apply spread costs. Report results with and without materials stocks. Size it as a satellite.
9. **Excluded strategies.** No short-term reversal or 52-week-high rules. No CFDs or leveraged products.
10. **Trend rules.** Label any trend or moving-average rule on index ETFs as unvalidated for Australia. Require out-of-sample testing and a comparison against buy-and-hold after costs and tax.
11. **Tax logic.** Track every parcel's days held around ex-dividend dates on a last-in-first-out basis. Flag any sale that would break the 45-day rule, and report year-to-date franking credits against the $5,000 threshold per person. Confirm the rules against the ATO pages before coding, because I could not open them.
12. **Benchmark.** Judge the system after costs and tax against a static VAS/VGS/VGAD mix at the chosen weights. If it does not beat that over a pre-agreed period, the fallback is the static mix.
13. **Open items.** Before finalising: read the ASIC and ATO primaries directly, confirm ASX 200 concentration from the S&P factsheet, and cover post-earnings drift only if single-stock strategies stay in scope.
